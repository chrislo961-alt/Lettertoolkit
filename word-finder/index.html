    How the filters work together

    Think of every field as another rule

    The strongest searches combine independent clues. Length defines the overall shape, a pattern fixes known positions, and starts/contains/ends/excludes remove candidates that cannot fit.

    1

    Set only the length you know

    Do not guess a length unless the puzzle gives you one. A wrong length removes the correct answer before any other filter is considered.

    2

    Use ? for unknown positions

    A pattern such as A??LE means five letters, beginning with A and ending with LE, while the two middle positions may vary.

    3

    Add positive and negative evidence

    If you know a word contains R but cannot contain S or T, enter R under Contains and ST under Exclude letters. Negative evidence can shrink a large list surprisingly fast.

    Worked examples

    From vague clue to useful shortlist

    Example 1: A??LE. If a five-letter puzzle gives A in position one and LE in positions four and five, enter length 5 and pattern A??LE. You do not need to repeat the same information in Starts with or Ends with unless it helps you keep track of the clue.

    Example 2: starts with ST, contains R, excludes AEI. This is useful when you know the opening letters and one internal letter, while previous guesses have ruled out several vowels. Each condition must be true at the same time, so the result set becomes much more focused than a simple “words starting ST” list.

    Example 3: crossword crossing letters. If crossings give _ A _ E R for a five-letter answer, enter ?A?ER. Then compare the candidates with the clue's meaning and grammar. A finder can match spelling constraints, but it cannot decide which candidate best fits the clue.

    Accuracy and word lists

    A spelling match is not always the final answer

    The finder searches LetterToolkit's English word dataset. Different dictionaries and games make different decisions about regional spellings, inflections, archaic words and specialist terms. Use uncommon matches as candidates to verify, particularly in competitive word games or schoolwork.
