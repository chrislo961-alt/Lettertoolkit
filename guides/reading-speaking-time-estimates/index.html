Timing guide

How reading and speaking time estimates work

A word count can be converted into a useful time estimate, but the result is never a stopwatch prediction. Pace changes with audience, difficulty, pauses, emphasis and familiarity with the material.

The basic formula

The calculation is simple: time in minutes = word count ÷ words per minute. A 1,000-word article at 200 words per minute is about five minutes of reading. A 650-word speech at 130 words per minute is about five minutes of speaking before extra pauses.

Why LetterToolkit uses different reading and speaking assumptions

Silent reading is generally faster than clear spoken delivery. LetterToolkit's main counter uses an approximate baseline of 200 words per minute for reading and 130 words per minute for speaking. These are transparent defaults for planning, not claims about every reader or speaker.

Example calculations

  • 300 words at 200 WPM → about 1.5 minutes of reading.
  • 800 words at 200 WPM → about 4 minutes of reading.
  • 390 words at 130 WPM → about 3 minutes of speaking.
  • 1,300 words at 130 WPM → about 10 minutes of speaking before deliberate pauses.

For user-facing displays, rounding upward can be more useful than showing a false level of precision. “About 4 minutes” is usually more meaningful than “3.87 minutes.”

What makes reading slower?

Technical terminology, unfamiliar names, dense arguments, equations, code and poorly structured paragraphs all slow comprehension. A reader scanning a familiar article may move much faster than someone studying a contract or textbook. Screen size and interruptions also matter.

What makes speaking slower?

Presentations need pauses. A speaker may stop for emphasis, wait for a slide, demonstrate something, take questions or allow an audience to react. Names and numbers can also take longer to say clearly. If a five-minute slot is strict, a script that mathematically fills all five minutes leaves no room for natural delivery.

Plan with a range, not one magic number

For important timing, calculate more than one pace. A speech could be checked at 110, 130 and 150 WPM to create slow, normal and fast scenarios. Reading estimates can likewise be compared at several speeds when the material is unusually simple or dense.

How to measure your own pace

  1. Choose a representative passage rather than your easiest paragraph.
  2. Read or speak it naturally while timing yourself.
  3. Divide its word count by the elapsed minutes.
  4. Repeat with another passage and average the results.
  5. Use that personal WPM for a more realistic estimate.

For speaking, rehearse aloud. Silent rehearsal does not capture breathing, articulation or pauses.

When an estimate is enough

Approximate timing is useful for blog posts, video scripts, presentations, lessons, podcast outlines and accessibility planning. Use a real rehearsal or measured reading test when timing is contractual, broadcast-critical or tied to a hard event schedule.
