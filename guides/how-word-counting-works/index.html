Text counting guide

How word counting works

“How many words is this?” sounds simple until punctuation, hyphens, numbers and unusual formatting enter the text. This guide explains a practical counting method and why two editors can sometimes disagree.

The basic rule

A practical word counter looks for meaningful sequences of letters or numbers separated by whitespace or punctuation. LetterToolkit keeps common internal apostrophes and hyphens inside a word, so don't and well-known are each treated as one word. Extra spaces, tabs and blank lines do not add words.

This is a useful general-purpose rule, not a universal publishing law. A university portal, newsroom or legal filing system may define a word differently.

Examples

Consider these short examples:

  • Hello world → 2 words.
  • Hello, world! → still 2 words because punctuation does not create an extra token.
  • don't stop → 2 words under LetterToolkit's apostrophe rule.
  • well-known author → 2 words under the hyphen rule.
  • 2026 plan → 2 words because a number can count as a token.
  • Several spaces or line breaks between the same two words do not change the count.

Why Microsoft Word, Google Docs and websites can disagree

Different products make different choices for compounds, em dashes, URLs, email addresses, emoji, symbols and non-Latin scripts. Some systems are optimized for publishing conventions while others use simpler token rules. A difference of one or two words does not automatically mean one counter is broken; it can mean the products use different definitions.

When a count matters for a formal limit, treat the destination system as the final authority. Draft with a consistent counter, then verify the final document in the submission platform.

Apostrophes and contractions

English contractions such as can't, we're and they've are normally understood as single written words, so keeping the apostrophe inside the token produces an intuitive result. Possessives such as student's are handled the same way.

Curly apostrophes copied from a word processor should behave like straight apostrophes. This is one reason a Unicode-aware counter is preferable to a rule that only understands basic ASCII letters.

Hyphenated words

There is no single convention that fits every context. LetterToolkit treats a common hyphenated sequence such as part-time as one word. A publisher may choose to count it differently. If you are working to a strict academic or editorial standard, check that standard before relying on any online counter.

Numbers, dates and mixed text

Numbers can carry meaning just like alphabetic words, so a standalone number such as 2026 is counted as a token. Punctuation around it normally does not add another word. Complex strings such as URLs, source-code snippets or formulas can be interpreted differently by different systems, so those are good cases for a final destination check.

Sentences and paragraphs are separate measurements

A word total does not tell you how readable a passage is. Sentence count, average sentence length, paragraph count and repeated-word frequency can reveal structure that a raw total cannot. LetterToolkit's Text Analyzer combines those measurements when you need more than a single number.

A repeatable workflow for strict limits

  1. Write and edit normally rather than cutting useful content too early.
  2. Use one counter consistently while drafting.
  3. Clean accidental repeated spaces or pasted formatting if needed.
  4. Check the final total in the system that enforces the limit.
  5. If the counts differ, inspect hyphenated terms, unusual punctuation, URLs and numbers first.
